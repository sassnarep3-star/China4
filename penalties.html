Penalties, precisely

What each unlawful route actually costs, quoted from the operative article rather than from commentary. Two things are worth saying at the outset: converting your own after-tax money lawfully attracts no tax penalty at all, and the most commonly repeated number for illegal FX trading — "30% to 300%" — is from the version of the regulations replaced in 2008.

None of these is a tax on moving money. They are penalties for not reporting, or for not declaring income. The FBAR figures are the inflation-adjusted maxima for penalties assessed on or after 17 January 2025; eCFR had not posted a later adjustment as of 2026-11-08.

The "30%–300%" figure is out of date

Older articles, and earlier drafts of this dashboard, quote a fine of "30% to 300%" for illegal FX trading. That range comes from the 1997 text of Art. 45, which set a fine of 30% up to three times the illegal amount (1997 FX Regulations text (MOJ)). The 2008 revision, in force today, caps the fine at 30% of the amount, rising to between 30% and the equivalent amount (i.e. 100%) in serious cases (Art. 45 of the 2008 text). The current range is therefore up to 30%, or 30%–100% if serious — still ruinous at six figures, but not 300%.

Non-monetary consequences

Watchlist for three years

Being listed for splitting or quota-borrowing means every later FX transaction needs documents, for the rest of that year and the next two. SAFE 汇发〔2015〕49号 (watchlist)

Frozen accounts

Funds traced to an illegal channel can be frozen or confiscated, and a US bank may exit the relationship when a foreign wire arrives with a murky source. Art. 39/45

Criminal liability

Both Art. 39 and Art. 45 provide that where a crime is constituted, criminal responsibility is pursued — the administrative fine is not the ceiling. FX Regulations 2008 (State Council)
