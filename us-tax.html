US tax and reporting

What a US citizen must report, what is actually taxable, and the currency gain most guides omit.

The distinction that matters

Moving your own savings across a border is not income. Three other things can still cost you:

  1. A currency gain on disposing of the RMB, taxable as ordinary income under IRC 988.
  2. Information-reporting penalties, which apply whether or not any tax is due.
  3. Chinese tax on the underlying income, if it was never declared in China.

What is and is not taxable

IDRuleExplanationAmountSource
T01Moving your own money between your own accounts is not a taxable eventNeither the FBAR nor Form 8938 is a tax; they are information reports. Repatriating savings you already owned does not create income merely because it crosses a border.—S15 S16
T02But disposing of RMB IS a section 988 transaction, and the gain is ORDINARY incomeIRC 988(c)(1)(C)(i) makes any disposition of nonfunctional currency a section 988 transaction, and (ii) expressly covers foreign-currency denominated demand or time deposits with a bank. Under 988(a)(1)(A) the resulting foreign currency gain or loss is treated as ordinary, not capital.
Statutory text
Section 988(c)(1)(C)(i): 'any disposition of nonfunctional currency'. Section 988(a)(1)(A): foreign currency gain or loss attributable to a section 988 transaction is computed separately and treated as ordinary income or loss.
—S19
T03There is a USD 200 personal-transaction exclusion — but it is gain-only and it is a cliffSection 988(e)(1) disapplies section 988 to an individual's personal transactions. Section 988(e)(2) then provides that no gain is recognised on a personal disposition of nonfunctional currency, UNLESS the gain that would otherwise be recognised exceeds USD 200. If the gain is USD 201, the whole gain is taxable, not just the excess. There is no corresponding relief for losses, because a personal loss is not deductible in the first place.
Statutory text
Section 988(e)(2): 'no gain shall be recognized for purposes of this subtitle by reason of changes in exchange rates after such currency was acquired by such individual and before such disposition. The preceding sentence shall not apply if the gain which would otherwise be recognized on the transaction exceeds $200.'
In practice: On a large repatriation, a multi-year RMB appreciation can easily produce a gain far above USD 200, so the exclusion will usually NOT save you. Compute the gain using your acquisition-date basis in USD, not the balance in RMB.
—S19
T04What counts as a 'personal transaction' is narrower than it soundsSection 988(e)(3) defines a personal transaction as any transaction entered into by an individual EXCEPT to the extent properly allocable expenses meet the requirements of section 162 (trade or business) or section 212 (production of income). An interest-bearing deposit held for investment is therefore not automatically a personal transaction.
Statutory text
Section 988(e)(3): 'the term "personal transaction" means any transaction entered into by an individual, except that such term shall not include any transaction to the extent that expenses properly allocable to such transaction meet the requirements of— (A) section 162 ..., or (B) section 212 ...'
—S19
T05The source of a section 988 gain follows your tax home, which affects foreign tax creditsSection 988(a)(3) sources foreign currency gain or loss by reference to the residence of the taxpayer, and for an individual residence is determined under the section 911(d)(3) tax-home concept. A US citizen whose tax home is in the US generally has US-source currency gain, which cannot be sheltered by foreign tax credits.—S19
T06The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion does not shelter a currency gain or a bank balanceThe FEIE applies only to foreign EARNED income — wages, salaries and professional fees — and expressly not to pensions, annuities or investment income. Repatriated savings are not earned income. For tax years beginning in 2026 the maximum exclusion is USD 132,900.USD 132,900

T07Form 8938 does not replace the FBAR — you may have to file bothThe IRS states expressly that filing Form 8938 does not relieve you of the requirement to file an FBAR. The two regimes have different thresholds, different filing destinations and different penalties.—S16 S15
T08Convert account values using the Treasury Bureau of the Fiscal Service year-end rateFor Form 8938 the IRS directs use of the Treasury's Financial Management Service / Bureau of the Fiscal Service foreign currency exchange rate as of the last day of the tax year. Using a random commercial rate is a common error.—S16
T09Omitting more than USD 5,000 of income from a foreign asset extends the statute of limitations to six yearsThe IRS FATCA summary states that failure to report more than USD 5,000 of income from a specified foreign financial asset extends the assessment period to six years. Combined with the 40% understatement penalty, non-disclosure is expensive well beyond the headline USD 10,000.—S16
T10US-China income tax treaty: 1984 treaty and technical explanationThe IRS treaty index lists the US-China income tax convention and its technical explanation, both dated 1984. The treaty can relieve double taxation on the underlying income, but it does not remove FBAR or FATCA reporting duties, because the treaty's information-exchange article contemplates exactly that reporting.
