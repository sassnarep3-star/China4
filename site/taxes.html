US tax obligations for money coming out of China

What a US citizen must report to the US Treasury and IRS when money moves from a Chinese bank account to the US. The act of moving your own money is not taxable — but failing to report it can be very expensive.

🚨 The most important fact first

US citizens and resident aliens are taxed on worldwide income, no matter where they live. However, transferring money you already own — including after-tax money from a Chinese bank account — is not a taxable event. There is no US “remittance tax” on bringing your own funds into the United States. The US obligations below are information reporting: FBAR, FATCA (Form 8938), and Form 3520 (for gifts). Miss them, and the penalties apply even if no tax is owed.

🗏 1. FBAR — Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FinCEN Form 114)

Item Rule Penalty if missed Source
Who must file US persons (citizens, green card holders, resident aliens, entities) with a financial interest in, or signature authority over, foreign financial accounts Non-willful: up to ~US$16,536 per violation (inflation-adjusted, 2026). Willful: greater of ~US$165,353 or 50% of the account balance per violation FinCEN — How do I file the FBAR?
Threshold Aggregate value of all foreign accounts exceeds US$10,000 at any time during the calendar year (not $10,000 per account; not just year-end)
Where / when Filed electronically via FinCEN BSA E-Filing (bsaefiling.fincen.gov) — not with the IRS tax return. Due April 15, automatic extension to October 15

If your Chinese account held more than US$10,000 (aggregate across all your foreign accounts) at any point, you must file an FBAR — even if you never move the money out of China.

🗏 2. FATCA — Form 8938, Statement of Specified Foreign Financial Assets

Item Rule Penalty if missed Source
Who must file Specified individuals required to file a US income tax return, holding specified foreign financial assets above the threshold US$10,000 per failure, plus up to US$10,000 per 30 days after IRS notice (capped at US$50,000); statute of limitations extended IRS — FATCA information for individuals · IRS Pub 54
Thresholds (2025) US residents: single >US$50,000 year-end or >US$75,000 any time; MFJ >US$100,000 / >US$150,000. Living abroad: single >US$200,000 / >US$300,000; MFJ >US$400,000 / >US$600,000
Where / when Attached to your Form 1040 tax return (same deadline, incl. extensions). Filing Form 8938 does not replace the FBAR — you may need both

🗏 3. Form 3520 — Foreign gifts and foreign trusts

Item Rule Penalty if missed Source
Who must file (Part IV) US person receiving gifts/bequests >US$100,000 in a year from foreign individuals or foreign estates (aggregate; gifts from related donors, e.g. both parents, are combined). Gifts from foreign corporations/partnerships: lower inflation-adjusted threshold (US$20,573 for 2026) 5% of the gift amount per month late, capped at 25%. Applies even if the gift itself is not taxable IRS — Form 3520 instructions (PDF)
Tax on the gift The gift itself is not US taxable income to the recipient. Only the reporting is mandatory.
Foreign trusts (Parts I–III) Any transaction with a foreign trust — no dollar threshold Greater of US$10,000 or 35% of the gross reportable amount IRS — Form 3520 instructions (PDF)

If your parents in China send you money: if it’s a genuine gift from parent(s) and the annual total exceeds US$100,000 (both parents combined), file Form 3520. If it’s a documented loan with a note and interest, it is not a gift — keep the paperwork.

🗏 4. If the money is income: foreign tax credit and exclusion

Item Rule Source
Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116) If you paid Chinese individual income tax (3–45% on comprehensive income) on the money, you can credit it against US tax on the same income, avoiding double taxation. Limited to the US tax attributable to that foreign income. IRS FAQ — international taxpayers
Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555) If you lived abroad and qualify, you may exclude up to US$130,000 (2025) of foreign earned income from US taxable income IRS Pub 54
US–China tax treaty (1984, in force 1987) Caps Chinese withholding at 10% on dividends/interest/royalties; contains a savings clause preserving US taxation of its citizens. Primary double-tax relief for most people is the foreign tax credit, not the treaty. IRS — China tax treaty documents (1984 PDF)

📌 Quick checklist: do I need to file anything?

  • Chinese account balance (with all foreign accounts) ever over US$10,000? → File FBAR (FinCEN Form 114).
  • Specified foreign financial assets above FATCA thresholds? → File Form 8938 with your 1040.
  • Received a gift over US$100,000 from a foreign person (e.g., parents in China) in one year? → File Form 3520.
  • Money is your own after-tax savings being moved to a US account? → No US tax on the transfer itself — but the account still counts toward FBAR/8938 thresholds.
  • Money is China-source income you never reported to the IRS? → Report it; claim foreign tax credit for Chinese tax paid. Consider the IRS Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures if you were non-willfully late.

🚩 What not to do

Do not try to hide the account: FBAR and Form 8938 penalties (up to ~US$16,536 non-willful per violation; willful penalties up to the greater of ~US$165,353 or 50% of the account balance) apply regardless of whether any tax is owed. If you are behind on filings, the IRS Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures (non-willful) and Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures can bring you into compliance with reduced or no penalties — act before the IRS contacts you.
