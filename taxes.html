US tax and reporting

The question behind the question: will the US tax me for bringing my own money home? The answer is no — but four separate reporting regimes sit around the transfer, and their penalties dwarf the cost of the transfer itself.

Moving your own money is not a taxable event

US citizens are taxed on worldwide income, not on the movement of capital. A wire from your Chinese account to your US account converts one form of your own property into another; it creates no income. What creates tax is the income that produced the money, any foreign-currency gain on the currency itself, and — for gifts — nothing at all on the recipient's side, though a large foreign gift must be reported. The currency-gain question

Foreign currency is property. The IRS's own practice unit on IRC §988 confirms that the section applies to "buying or selling units of foreign currency", and that exchange gain or loss is computed using the spot rate under Treas. Reg. 1.988-1(d). IRS practice unit on IRC 988 In practice, for someone who received RMB as salary and simply converted it, the gain or loss is usually small or nil — but the basis and holding period have to be known, which is another reason to keep the bank records.

Gifts, bequests and the donor's side

A gift from a Chinese relative is not taxable income to you: the US gift tax, where it applies at all, is imposed on the donor, and a nonresident alien donor's gift of non-US assets is generally outside it. The obligation that does bite is informational — Form 3520, Part IV, once gifts from one foreign individual (aggregating related donors) exceed US$100,000 in a year. IRS 'gifts from foreign person' · IRS Form 3520 instructions (12/2025)

Two traps: the threshold is per year and per donor group, and the China side does not care that the US treats it as a gift — the donor's RMB still has to be converted lawfully, and marshalling five or more people to remit to you is precisely the pattern SAFE prohibits. SAFE 汇发〔2015〕49号 (watchlist)

Unverified: the precise interaction between the US gift-tax situs rules and a nonresident donor's gift of funds held in a Chinese bank was not traced to an official statement in this session. Treaty, credit and double taxation

The 1984 US–China income tax treaty allocates taxing rights and provides relief mechanisms, and — importantly — contains a savings clause preserving the US right to tax its own citizens. US–China tax treaty (IRS) Where China has already taxed the same income, the foreign tax credit under Form 1116 is the ordinary relief; the foreign earned income exclusion is a separate, narrower relief for earned income. IRS Publication 54

Note that a foreign tax credit requires the foreign tax to have been paid. A plan that avoids Chinese tax therefore does not create a credit — it creates US tax on the same income.

Records to keep

  • Bank statements showing the RMB balance and its history.
  • The conversion advice and the wire confirmation, showing the rate, fees and the receiving account.
  • Source-of-funds documents: employment contract and payslips, tax payment certificates, property contract and tax clearance, or the estate documents.
  • Copies of every US information return filed (FBAR, 8938, 3520) and the underlying account valuations.

The FBAR rules require records for five years from the due date. IRS FBAR page
